Monday, November 9 (US Eastern). Oil dropped hard again, and the S&P 500 had its best session since April. All eleven sectors rose, and the chip stocks that got hammered hardest last week bounced hardest. The post-mortems sang in unison: oil is down, inflation fears are cooling, the alarm is off, everyone can exhale.

But one market refused to exhale. The two-year Treasury yield, as published by the US Treasury, went *up* on Monday. Scroll back to the end of October, when oil was still above $100 a barrel: the two-year was at 4.56%. On Monday it closed at 4.56%. Not an inch given. Oil kept tumbling, and the rate that sits closest to the Fed simply stood still. **Two markets looked at the same barrel of oil and came to opposite conclusions.** So which one is misreading it?

Let me put the conclusion up front; every section below explains it: **falling oil tears up the IOUs that haven't been written yet. It does not tear up the ones already signed.**

## 1. A price sign and a ledger

Oil made a U-turn like this [in early August](/en/posts/2026-08-04-barrel-of-oil/), and the market cheered like this then too; that piece argued that one day's oil price is not the whole road of inflation. This one pushes a step further. The question is not whether to watch oil, but why, once oil turns back, the ledger doesn't turn back with it.

The stock market is watching the price sign at the gas station: the sign flips down, so inflation should retreat and the case for a hike should weaken. At the first layer, that's right. The sign does change first, and in the next price report, energy will very likely move down.

But the two-year yield isn't reading a price sign. It's reading a balance. Its meaning is plain: how much interest do you demand to lend the government money for two years? The Fed's policy range is 3.75% to 4% today, yet the two-year demands 4.56%. **Lenders insist on getting more than half a percentage point above today's rate, which can only mean one thing: they believe rates over the next two years will be higher than today's.** Oil has fallen this far, and not a cent of that pre-paid hike has been refunded.

That explains what each market is looking at. It doesn't explain the other thing: if oil has fallen, what exactly is the front end still afraid of?

## 2. The IOUs were written long ago

[Last week's piece](/en/posts/wages-are-an-iou/) argued that wages are not inflation's accelerator; they are the IOU inflation wrote. Follow that IOU down the page, and the answer is on the ledger.

In September, headline prices were up 3.7% from a year earlier; strip out food and energy and core was up just 2.6%. Energy alone rose by a fifth over the year. Nearly all of that gap of more than a percentage point was propped open by oil. But over that year, oil didn't just push up the price at the pump. It wrote IOU after IOU: workers asked for catch-up raises, landlords nudged rents up at renewal, freight contracts were re-signed at high-oil prices, companies printed new price lists.

These IOUs share one trait: **easy to write, hard to tear up.** Wage contracts don't get revised down because oil got cheaper. Menus don't get reprinted because diesel fell. A one-year lease waits a year to be renegotiated. Oil is a flow: down today, up tomorrow. The IOUs are a stock: once signed, they sit on the books.

So what this slide actually tears up is only the IOUs that *would have been written* if oil had kept climbing. The ones already out there? Not one is gone.

## 3. Up and down are not the same road

Put the climb and the slide side by side, and the asymmetry jumps off the page.

**When oil rises:** (1) the pump moves first, and headline prices jump; (2) workers and firms see prices rising, and start pushing up wages and price lists; (3) those new prices get written into contracts and become next year's starting point. **The rise itself writes IOUs.**

**When oil falls:** (1′) the pump moves first, and headline prices ease; (2′) but wages don't get cut, price lists don't get withdrawn, leases don't get rewritten; (3′) whatever was written into contracts stays exactly where it is. **The fall only stops new IOUs from being written. It doesn't pay off the old ones.**

The same oil price leaves carrying a string of IOUs and comes back empty-handed. **That is why headline inflation follows oil while core inflation only answers to the IOUs.** And when the Fed decides whether to hike, it's the latter it watches.

But there's a more awkward layer: could the stock market's cheering itself become a new IOU?

## 4. The cheering is an IOU too

It could. This is where the system turns reflexive.

When stocks rip on "inflation is cooling," household paper wealth gets a little thicker, corporate financing gets a little looser, and the confidence to spend gets a little stronger. **Cheering that inflation is cooling is itself heating up demand.** The Fed has never looked at oil alone; it looks at the whole web of financial conditions: equity prices, credit spreads, the dollar, borrowing costs. Loosen the web and demand has room to keep expanding, and the old IOUs have fertile ground to be rolled into new ones.

So the two-year not giving an inch isn't the bond market "failing to understand oil." Quite the opposite: it has priced in the stock market's cheering too. What the front end is really saying is cold and simple: **oil can flip the sign at the pump, but not the balance. And your cheering is still being booked to the balance.**

## 5. Tonight's statement

How do we check? Tonight at 8:30 a.m. US Eastern, the October CPI comes out. One thing is close to a foregone conclusion: the headline will look better as gasoline eases. What actually matters are two things: **whether the services and rent pieces of core prices loosen, and which way the two-year yield moves once the numbers land.** Pretty headline, sticky core, two-year flat: the IOUs are still there. Core loosening too, with the two-year following it down: the books are actually being cleared.

Now the one place where I have to haircut my confidence: direction. I'm about 60% sure, not 90%, that the front end will not give back its hike pricing because of oil before the December meeting. What being wrong looks like is concrete: if, on December 9, the day of the FOMC decision, the two-year yield has fallen below 4.51%, where it stood on November 6 before oil's big drop, the market will have concluded the IOUs are being cleared, and I'll have been wrong.

The framework has an edge, too. If oil is falling not because supply loosened but because demand is genuinely cracking, then it's not "no new IOUs." It's the economy actively defaulting on the old ones: wages fall, layoffs arrive, leases soften, and the ratchet gets snapped by force. In that world the front end falls right along with oil, and that would not be good news.

## 6. The sign can flip; the ledger can't

In the end, people love watching the sign at the pump because it's simple: one number, changes every day, readable at a glance. But inflation isn't a number. It's a web. Oil, wages, rents, and margins push against each other inside it: inseparable, interacting, reflexive. In that web, whatever moves first is easiest to see, and whatever clears last is easiest to forget.

There has never been a button in the market that resets the ledger to zero. Oil has merely changed direction at the web's entrance; every IOU on the books still has to be reconciled, one by one, and paid, one by one. Those rushing to declare the alarm off are watching the entrance. The ones not giving an inch are watching the ledger.

<p class="kicker">Oil fell, the IOU is still there. However fast the sign at the pump flips, it can't flip the balance on the books.</p>
