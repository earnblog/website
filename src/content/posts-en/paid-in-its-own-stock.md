A customer warrant is not accounting noise. It is a discount paid in stock.

After the close on November 3 (US Eastern), AMD posted a quarter that was hard to fault: data center revenue nearly doubled year over year, and next-quarter guidance came in around $17 billion, well above what the Street had penciled in. The stock rose more than 7% after hours. Before the call was even over, a consensus had formed: **the only ugly number, GAAP profit, was dragged down by a "change in fair value of customer warrants." No cash leaves the building, so strip it out and look at adjusted earnings.** In the models, that line was neatly crossed out.

Let me put the conclusion up front, and the rest of this piece will just explain it: **that line is not noise. It is part of the price.** The chips AMD sells to its biggest customer carry a sticker price in dollars, but the transaction price has a share of AMD stock folded inside it. What that share is worth depends on the stock price. Which means AMD's stock price is not just its report card. It is printed on its price list.

## 1. What exactly is the $1.1 billion?

Start with the books. This quarter the change in fair value of customer warrants came to about $1.1 billion, cutting GAAP EPS by $0.66, roughly a third of adjusted earnings. The gap between the GAAP and adjusted ledgers is almost entirely this one line. Last quarter and a year ago, the line was blank. This is the first time it has been big enough to need its own adjustment.

AMD's explanation is a single sentence: the charge reflects the quarterly remeasurement of "warrants issued to certain customers," driven mainly by AMD's stock price.

In plain English: AMD owes a customer some stock. When the stock goes up, that IOU gets more expensive, and the increase is booked as an expense. **The more successful the company, the more it "loses" on paper.** It sounds like accounting theater. It is actually the most honest line in the whole report.

So the next question: why would a chip company owe its customer stock at all?

## 2. Not a freebie, a rebate

The answer is in a filing from a year ago. In October 2025, AMD signed a phased GPU deployment deal with OpenAI and issued it a warrant to buy up to 160 million AMD shares at one cent each. The shares do not arrive at once. They vest in tranches: each time OpenAI buys another block of AMD GPUs, another tranche unlocks, and the final tranche also requires AMD's stock to reach $600.

Take the terms apart and you have **a purchase contract with an equity rebate.** For every truckload you buy, I hand you a bundle of my company's shares. The bundle isn't a gift. It is money flowing back out of the invoice, paid not in dollars but in ownership.

So the real transaction price of a chip is not the number on the invoice. It is **the invoice, minus whatever the newly unlocked shares are worth.** Adjusted earnings strip out that line, which is the same as reading the sticker and ignoring the price actually paid. "Non-cash" is true. But non-cash is not free. What AMD spends is its shareholders' slice of the company, and every cent of it dilutes the profit attached to each future share.

None of this is AMD's invention. Amazon has collected similar warrants from more than one supplier over the years, and in the past two years chipmakers taking stakes in their largest customers, who then spend that money on chips, has become routine. The mechanism is everywhere. What differs at AMD are the parameters: larger scale, tighter coupling to one customer's purchase milestones, and a stock-price hurdle on top.

Calling the warrant a discount, though, only says what it is. It doesn't answer the harder question: why does the discount move with the stock?

## 3. The stock price is on the price list

Because the discount is denominated in AMD's stock. Follow that thread and you get a textbook reflexive loop.

On the way up: ① AMD's stock rises; ② the customer's warrant gains value, so the same truckload of chips effectively costs the customer less; ③ the more it buys, the more it unlocks, and the stronger the pull to keep ordering, and those orders prop up revenue and guidance, which prop up the stock. **The rally itself cuts the price of AMD's chips, and the price cut helps fund the rally.**

On the way down: ①′ the stock falls; ②′ the warrant shrinks, the customer's effective cost quietly rises, and the final price hurdle drifts out of reach, turning the rebate into a picture of a cake; ③′ the marginal appeal of buying fades, and once ordering slows, revenue and guidance lose speed, and the stock takes another hit. **The decline itself raises the price of AMD's chips.**

In that sense, the "loss" in GAAP earnings is not noise. It is the gauge on this loop: **every step higher in the stock is another slice of discount handed to the customer, and the accountants simply wrote it down.** Strip it out and the numbers look cleaner, but what you've really done is unscrew the dashboard.

If that line is a gauge, what can a reader read off it?

## 4. Watch the spread, not the sticker

Two things are worth watching.

The first is the gap between GAAP and adjusted earnings. It is not one-off. As long as the warrant hasn't fully vested and the stock keeps moving, it will come back every quarter, pointing the same way as the stock: wide open in a quarter when the stock soars, and in a quarter when the stock falls, it can even flip and add to GAAP profit. That isn't the business getting better; it is the stock owed to the customer getting cheaper. **Anyone who cheers a GAAP improvement in a down quarter is mistaking a shrinking discount for better operations.**

The second is the race between purchase milestones and the price hurdle. How fast the customer buys GPUs and how high the stock climbs decide how many of those 160 million shares eventually unlock. When the two lines run together, the loop turns smoothly. When one falls behind, the loop starts to stall.

The framework has limits. A good chunk of that nearly doubled data center revenue comes from server CPUs and other customers that have nothing to do with the warrant; reading the whole report as "stock for orders" is its own overreach. And the warrant is capped: however high the stock goes, the discount has a ceiling, so the loop cannot amplify forever. The framework describes the arithmetic of one big customer at the margin, not the pricing of every chip.

## 5. A price is never one number

In the end, the market's favorite shortcut is compressing a complicated price into one clean number. Revenue is a number, adjusted EPS is a number, guidance is a number, and everything labeled "non-cash," "one-time" or "other" goes into the noise bin.

But a real deal is never one number. It is a whole contract: sticker, rebate, hurdle, timing, each one wired to the others, each one reflexive. In the contract between AMD and its biggest customer, the most important variable isn't even in the contract. It is on the stock screen, every day. The line you deleted is exactly where the system is telling you how it keeps its books. **Humility before a complex system starts with not rushing to delete its numbers for it.**
