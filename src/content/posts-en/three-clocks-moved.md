This week the two worst corners of the US stock market were chips and oil. The PHLX Semiconductor Index lost about 12% on the week; energy stocks lost about 12% too, almost to the decimal. One is the industry with the brightest future, the other is the commodity that has been scarcest right now. They have nothing to do with each other, yet in the same five days they took the same deep cut. Meanwhile gold rose, payrolls blew past expectations, and hike bets climbed.

Most recaps split the week into three stories: after the election, data center buildouts will be slowed; the energy squeeze may ease; the job market is too hot, so the Fed may hike. Three stories, three explanations, each talking past the others. But when three things happen in the same week and produce nearly identical damage, it is worth asking whether they are really one thing. **My answer: this week the market was not trading news. It was trading the schedule.** Every section below is an explanation of that sentence.

## 1. The thread: three clocks, moved at once

A price never buys whether the future arrives. It buys when the future arrives.

Start with chips. On Thursday, November 5 (US Eastern), the semiconductor index fell about 12% in one session; almost the whole week's loss came that day. Yet on that day no company cut an order, no data center project was formally canceled, and not a single restriction had taken effect. Only one thing changed: **the market's estimate of when compute demand turns into revenue moved later.** That was the point of Friday's piece, [Delay Is a Markdown](/en/posts/delay-is-a-markdown/): a growth stock's valuation is not a number but a schedule. The same order is worth much less today if it lands the year after next instead of next year, and the more expensive time is, the wider that gap.

Now energy. Brent crude fell roughly 16% on the week, and energy stocks fell about 12% with it. Part of the oil price is a bet on how long the squeeze lasts. When the market wagers that tight supply will end sooner than expected, that premium has to be given back all at once. **Chips saw the future pushed back; oil saw the squeeze pulled to an earlier end. They look like opposite moves, but they are the same move: a schedule was rewritten, and prices are quoted off the schedule.**

The third clock is rates. Friday's October payrolls crushed expectations, the 2-year yield jumped within the day, and December hike bets were pulled forward. For long-dated assets that is salt in the wound: the discount rate, the haircut you apply when you bring future money back to today, gets more expensive sooner.

Put the three clocks together and the week takes shape:

There is a reflexive loop here worth spelling out. Falling chip stocks rewrite the chip schedule themselves: (1) prices fall, so data center projects funded by stock and bond sales become more expensive to finance; (2) costlier financing actually delays some of those projects; (3) the delays then seem to prove the selloff was justified. **Prices do not just read the schedule. They rewrite it.** The mirror image runs the same way: (1′) prices rise, so funding is cheap; (2′) cheap funding pulls projects forward; (3′) earlier projects mean earlier orders, and the stock rises with more conviction. Much of chips' run over the past two years came from this clock being wound forward. This week it was wound back hard for the first time.

In that sense, the popular line this week, "fundamentals haven't changed by a word, it's just sentiment," is half right. Today's profits really have not changed. What changed is when those profits arrive. And for assets priced off a schedule, **time is the fundamental.**

That explains chips and oil. It does not explain something else: in a week when hike bets rose, why did the 10-year yield fall and gold rally? For that we have to go to the data.

## 2. The week in data and markets

**Stocks.** The S&P 500 fell 2.7% and the Nasdaq Composite 3.4% on the week (October 30 close to November 6 close, US Eastern). By Thursday's close the Nasdaq was more than 10% below its high, the usual definition of a correction; Friday's bounce did not change the week's direction. The index fell far less than chips, which tells you this was not "sell everything" but selective selling: what got sold was the slice most dependent on distant cash flows, and chips were first in line.

**Treasuries.** The 10-year yield fell 8 basis points on the week to close at 4.86%. Eight basis points is less than a tenth of a percentage point. Yet the 2-year rose 5 basis points on Friday alone after payrolls. Short end up, long end down: that is the "hikes pulled forward" clock and the "squeeze ends sooner" clock in a tug-of-war on the same curve. The first pushes up rates for the next year or two; the second lowers inflation expectations for the next ten. Cheaper energy means bondholders need less compensation for inflation over a decade.

**Gold and oil.** Gold futures rose about 4.5% on the week; Brent fell roughly 16%. Two commodities went in almost opposite directions. What gold bought this week was the unreliability of schedules themselves: when three clocks move at once, the asset with no cash flows, and therefore no schedule, becomes one of the few things that cannot be rescheduled.

**Bitcoin.** Bitcoin (Coinbase price) slipped a little under 2% on the week, ending around $76,000; it spiked midweek and was knocked back. It traded more like a risk asset than a haven: bouncing and fading with the Nasdaq, only with much smaller swings.

**Macro data.** The week's key release was the October jobs report on November 6 (US Eastern): payrolls rose 165,000 and private-sector average hourly earnings rose 0.4% in the month. Easier to miss were the revisions: August and September together were revised up by 84,000, more than the average monthly gain of the past year. The report pulled hike bets forward, but Saturday's piece, [Wages Are an IOU](/en/posts/wages-are-an-iou/), argued that this round of wage gains is chasing oil, not driving inflation. Tuesday's CPI is the first statement on that account.

## 3. Calls in review

**This week's six pieces, one line each:**

- Nov 3, [the liquidity piece](/en/posts/the-other-side-of-the-trade/): liquidity is not water in a pool but someone willing to take the other side, and it disappears exactly when you need it.
- Nov 3, [the October data pack](/en/posts/datapack-2026-10/) (research tier): energy opened a gap of more than a percentage point between headline and core inflation, and the front of the curve flipped from "waiting for cuts" to "waiting for hikes."
- Nov 4, [the AMD warrants piece](/en/posts/paid-in-its-own-stock/): customer warrants are not accounting noise but a discount paid in AMD's own stock.
- Nov 5, [the bill piece](/en/posts/the-bill-has-two-ends/): AI capex is a bill; one side's cost is the other side's revenue, and a bill cannot be good news at both ends.
- Nov 6, [the markdown piece](/en/posts/delay-is-a-markdown/): a growth stock's valuation is a schedule, delay is a markdown, and this one will not be erased this year.
- Nov 7, [the IOU piece](/en/posts/wages-are-an-iou/): wages are chasing an IOU written by oil, and core inflation will not be dragged higher by them.

**The three calls that came due this week:**

- **Oct 2, the "rent on time" 10-year piece: partly right.** I said the 10-year would hold above 5% and that softer inflation data would not pull it down. By November 2 it was at 4.91%. **That half was wrong**: yields could come down. The right half was the mechanism: over that month the drop came mainly from real yields, not inflation expectations, so "rent" really was the main actor. Where did I go wrong? I read who was pushing correctly but thought the push would last too long. That is itself a schedule error, the same kind of mistake as this week's theme.
- **Oct 3, "the first print is only a first quote": right.** September payrolls first came in at +29,000 and were revised up by 31,000, just clearing the 30,000 bar. Honestly, it cleared by a hair; but the bigger revision was in August, and taken together the direction matches the call: a first print is only a first quote.
- **Oct 6, "one rack counted twice": right.** In Q3, all three big tech companies with cloud businesses grew capex faster than cloud revenue. The widest gap was Amazon: spending on equipment more than doubled from a year earlier (+120%), while AWS revenue grew less than 40% (+39%). The narrowest was Google, with only about ten points between them. If Google Cloud keeps accelerating like this next quarter, that is where this call's footing loosens first.

## 5. Stepping back: the clocks are in no one's hands

Looking back at this week, the easiest mistake is to go looking for the person who moved the clocks: the election, some firm's estimate, a jobs report. But the clocks are in no one's hands. Each one is the sum of countless participants' estimates of when the future arrives, colliding and correcting. No one can order them to run faster or slower. All anyone can do is notice, a little earlier than others, that they have moved.

The lesson this week, and I mean it for myself first: **calling direction is easy; calling timing is hard.** My early-October call that the 10-year would hold above 5% got the mechanism right and the timing wrong, and the market rescheduled it within a month. Prices never answer only "whether." They answer "when." And on that second question, no one has the right to be too sure.

<p class="kicker">The market trades the schedule, not the news, and prices do not just read the schedule. They keep rewriting it.</p>
