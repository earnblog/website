On Friday, November 6 (US Eastern), the October jobs report landed with 165,000 new payrolls. Over the previous twelve months, the US had been adding just 48,000 jobs a month on average; this single month did the work of more than three. Wages were even louder: private-sector average hourly earnings rose 0.4% in one month, roughly one and a half times the average monthly pace of the past year.

The market's reaction was close to a reflex: more workers, higher pay, inflation about to be dragged higher, a stronger case for a December hike. Treasury yields rose on the day and hike bets climbed another notch. The reading is tidy, every link locks into the next, and it sounds airtight. But it rests on something nobody ever proved: **that wages are in front and inflation follows behind.** What if the order runs the other way?

Wages are not inflation's accelerator. They are the IOU inflation wrote.

Start with the order. Private hourly pay rose 3.1% over the past year. Over the same stretch, consumer prices rose 3.7%; that's September's CPI, since October's isn't out until next Tuesday. Pay lost the race by more than half a point. The Bureau of Labor Statistics does this arithmetic itself: after prices, what an ordinary employee earns per hour buys 0.7% less than a year ago. Picture it as a grocery bag: the same hour of work that filled the bag last year now leaves the scallions on the shelf. **Wages are not the runner out in front of inflation. They are the one left behind, chasing to collect what is owed.**

That settles who is first and who is second. It doesn't yet say who ran out ahead.

Split the 3.7% and the answer is almost obvious. Energy rose faster than anything else this year; the price board at the gas station seemed to change every month. Strip out food and energy and core prices rose only 2.6%. The gap of more than a point between headline and core is almost entirely oil. So this IOU wasn't written by workers to themselves. **It was written by oil: energy took a slice out of the worker's pocket first, and wages are now coming back for that slice.** October's jump looks like a foot on the accelerator. It is actually an installment on the IOU.

But the important thing about an IOU is never the amount. It's who pays it. And that is where the whole story forks.

The first road: employers absorb it. Wages are catching up, but what companies sell hasn't risen as much: 3.1% wage growth is running ahead of 2.6% core inflation. So (1) labor costs rise faster than selling prices and margins get squeezed; (2) squeezed margins make firms pickier about hiring; (3) slower hiring takes some leverage out of workers' hands, and the catch-up slows. That's negative feedback, a system repairing itself: **the IOU is paid out of employers' margins, and once it's paid, the books are square.**

The second road runs the other way: employers pass it on. (1′) Seeing wages rise, firms simply raise their prices to match; (2′) prices climb another round and workers fall behind again; (3′) workers chase again, firms raise again, each foot pushing off the other, higher with every step. That's positive feedback, a system reinforcing itself: **the IOU never gets paid. It gets rolled over into a bigger one.** The wage-price spiral of the 1970s was, at bottom, this IOU being rolled over again and again.

Both roads start from the same place: "wages are rising." What separates them is not how fast wages rise but whether firms dare to, and can, pass the cost on. In that sense, staring at one month's jump in hourly pay is staring at the wrong thing. It tells you the IOU is being paid. It doesn't tell you who is paying.

So what should you watch? Core prices. Whether wages can drag inflation higher won't be answered by the jobs report, but by core inflation over the next few months. If core holds around two and a half, employers are absorbing the IOU. If wages pull core higher, the spiral has actually begun. Next Tuesday's October CPI and the November CPI on December 10 are the IOU's next two statements.

Here is the one place that deserves a discount: direction. I lean toward the first road, with about 60% conviction, not 90%. The reasons: core inflation has actually drifted lower over the past year, so firms' pricing power hasn't shown up; and this hiring rebound came mainly from professional services and health care, not from the industries that can reprice at will. What being wrong looks like is specific: if core CPI for November runs above 2.8% year over year, or if wage growth accelerates instead of easing and overtakes headline inflation, the spiral is starting and I'll own it.

The framework has its own failure mode, too. If oil refuses to come down and both workers and employers start writing "three or four percent a year" into contracts as the new normal, expectations will roll the IOU over on their own. At that point the order no longer matters; front and back have bitten into a single loop. The framework can tell you who moved first. It can't tell you when people stop believing that things will go back.

In the end, this is a small lesson in humility about cause and effect. We like to picture inflation as someone pressing an accelerator, workers asking too much or companies being greedy, because once you find the person with a foot on the pedal, the story is over. But prices are a network. Energy, wages, rent and profits push against one another inside it, inseparable, interacting, reflexive. In that network, whoever moves last is the easiest to blame. And workers are almost always the ones who move last.

<p class="kicker">Wages are not inflation's accelerator. They are the IOU inflation wrote, and what matters is not how big the IOU is, but who ends up paying it.</p>
