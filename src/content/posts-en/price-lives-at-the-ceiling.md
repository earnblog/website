**A guidance range isn't a midpoint. It's a ceiling.**

On Tuesday, October 20 (Eastern), General Motors reported its third quarter: revenue of about $50 billion, and a full-year EBIT-adjusted profit outlook revised from "$14 billion to $16 billion" to "$14.5 billion to $15.5 billion." Each end moved in by half a billion; the midpoint stayed parked at $15 billion, not a cent lower. The midpoint of its earnings-per-share guidance actually inched up. Yet the stock closed down 6.84% that day — on a day the S&P 500 finished slightly higher.

The popular reading: a good report got punished; the midpoint didn't move, so the market overreacted. **That reading assumes one thing — that price is set off the midpoint.** But price never lives in the middle of the range. It lives right under the roof. A narrower range looks like "more certainty." To price, it looks like someone took a pair of scissors to the exact floor it was living on. This piece answers one question: if the midpoint didn't move, why did price have to move out?

Start with a mirror. One year earlier, on October 21, 2025 (Eastern) — same company, same third-quarter report — GM also narrowed its range, taking the same profit measure from "$10 billion to $12.5 billion" to "$12 billion to $13 billion." Notice that the new floor, $12 billion, sat almost on top of the old ceiling, $12.5 billion. **The whole range was lifted onto the old roof.** The stock rose nearly 15% that day.

Two narrowings: one up fifteen percent, one down almost seven. The difference isn't whether the range narrowed, and it isn't whether the midpoint moved. It's where the ceiling went. A year ago, the ceiling was raised, and the floor price lived on became the new ground floor. This year, the ceiling was pushed from $16 billion down to $15.5 billion, and the floor price lived on was demolished. **Price doesn't buy the midpoint. It buys the ceiling.**

That explains what happened, not why. Why does price insist on living right under the roof? The answer is a loop that feeds itself. ① Companies guide with room to spare, preferring to under-promise and beat later. ② The market, after a few rounds of "beats," learns to read every range from the top. ③ Once price is set off the top, management has to deliver something near the top, or it's a "miss." ④ So the next time, the company leaves even more room, and the market reads even higher. **The midpoint is the polite fiction both sides know about; the ceiling is the quote both sides quietly agree on.** No one decreed that the market should read guidance this way. It's an order that grew out of countless rounds of the same game.

Stretch the calendar and this order wears two faces in a single year. In January: ①′ four quarters still to go, so the range is wide; ②′ the ceiling stands for imagination — new models, falling costs, easing tariffs, anything could happen; ③′ the higher a wide range's ceiling, the more future price can pay for in advance. By the third quarter: ① nine months are already on the books, with EBIT-adjusted totaling about $12.3 billion; ② the full-year range now has only one variable left — the fourth quarter; ③ the ceiling is no longer imagination, it's arithmetic. At the old $16 billion ceiling, the fourth quarter could still bring about $3.7 billion; at the new $15.5 billion ceiling, only about $3.2 billion — my own subtraction from the company's figures, not a quarterly guide GM issued. **In January, the ceiling is an option. By October, the option has expired.** Narrowing the range isn't bad news in itself; it's the routine paperwork of expiry. What hurts is that at the moment of expiry, the ceiling landed below where price was standing.

In that sense, the most important number in the October 20 release wasn't the $50 billion of revenue, and it wasn't the unmoved midpoint. It was the ceiling cut from $16 billion to $15.5 billion. The first is a report card. The second is a change-of-address notice for price.

This framework has its blind spots. GM's quarter wasn't only about trimming the ceiling: after the release, the market was also weighing the quality of the fourth quarter itself and the pressure on costs. How much of the drop was "ceiling cut" and how much was worry about the coming quarter can't be cleanly separated from one day's move. And the more people read guidance this way, the more companies will set their ceilings lower in advance, and the gap will be priced away early — at which point the ceiling stops being the quote and goes back to being a polite fiction.

So what can an ordinary reader watch? Next time you read guidance, don't start by computing the midpoint. Put the new ceiling next to the old one: was it raised, or pushed down? Then ask how many months the range still has to run — the fewer left, the less the ceiling is a vision and the more it is arithmetic.

In the end, this is a small lesson in humility about certainty. We instinctively believe a narrower range is better, that certainty is worth more. But the market, a system computed jointly by countless people, bought the most valuable slice of uncertainty — the upside slice — long ago. The moment certainty arrives is often the moment that slice is taken back.

<p class="kicker">Price never lives at the midpoint; it lives right under the ceiling, and when the ceiling comes down, it has to move.</p>
