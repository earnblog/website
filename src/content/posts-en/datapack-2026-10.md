This is the research tier's monthly data pack, October 2026 edition. It doesn't tell a story. It takes four sets of data I use every day, pulls them from primary sources, charts them, tables them, and runs them through the same mechanism: CPI components (the BLS September report, released in October), the US Treasury yield curve (end of October), spot bitcoin ETF flows (monthly), and capex at the four big cloud providers (quarterly, now through Q3 2026). Every number and every definition is listed at the end.

The biggest change this month fits in one sentence: headline and core inflation have parted ways, energy is what pushed them apart, and the short end of the bond market has already changed its answer.

## The month in brief

Headline CPI rose to 3.74% year on year in September, while core sat at just 2.56%. Nearly all of the gap of more than a point is energy. Core isn't calm, though: shelter, services and goods all ran faster month on month than in August, just nowhere near energy's pace. Over the past three months the short end of the Treasury curve rose faster than the long end, and the 2s10s spread narrowed from 0.47 to 0.38 percentage points. Over a full year, the very front of the curve has flipped from sloping down to sloping up. Spot bitcoin ETFs saw a small net outflow in October, and the past twelve months net out negative. The four hyperscalers spent a combined $201.9 billion on capex in Q3, more than twice what they spent a year earlier.

## 1. Inflation components: energy pushes, core follows

<figure class="bls-diagram"><img src="/datapack/2026-10/inflation.svg" alt="Line chart of CPI components year on year, September 2024 to September 2026: headline CPI, core CPI, shelter, services ex-energy and core goods; headline climbs clearly after March 2026 while core moves sideways around 2.5%"/><figcaption>CPI components, year on year (not seasonally adjusted). Energy is running around 20% and would flatten every other line on the same chart, so it's left out here; its numbers are in the table. The data has no October 2025 reading.</figcaption></figure>

| Component | Sep 2026 YoY | Sep 2026 MoM (SA) | Aug 2026 YoY | Sep 2025 YoY |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headline CPI | 3.74% | 0.65% | 3.40% | 3.01% |
| Core CPI | 2.56% | 0.33% | 2.45% | 3.02% |
| Shelter | 3.22% | 0.39% | 3.04% | 3.58% |
| Services ex-energy | 3.15% | 0.37% | 3.01% | 3.47% |
| Core goods | 0.74% | 0.25% | 0.68% | 1.54% |
| Energy | 20.45% | 5.20% | 16.28% | 2.85% |

**What's pushing: energy.** Up 5.2% in a single month, with the annual rate jumping from 16.28% in August to 20.45%. A year ago it was 2.85%, and headline and core were nearly identical (3.01% vs 3.02%). Today they're 1.18 points apart. The gap between headline and core inflation is, at bottom, a gap that oil prices pried open.

**What's holding back: core goods.** At 0.74% year on year it's the lowest line in the table, half the 1.54% of a year ago. Core CPI is lower than it was a year ago (3.02%), and so are shelter and services, because these legs haven't been dragged up by oil yet. They're the quietest part of the table.

**What changed from last month: core started to follow.** All four core month-on-month readings were faster in September than in August: core 0.29% → 0.33%, shelter 0.26% → 0.39%, services 0.33% → 0.37%, core goods 0.11% → 0.25%. None of them is alarming on its own, but when all four move up together it isn't noise in a single category. Energy rises first; then freight, travel and goods costs pass it on. That's the "second-round effect": the first round is oil itself, the second is oil turning into someone else's cost. September is the first month the second round has shown its face. It isn't confirmed yet.

## 2. The Treasury curve: same starting point, opposite slope

<figure class="bls-diagram"><img src="/datapack/2026-10/yields.svg" alt="Three Treasury yield curves, end of October 2026, end of July 2026 and end of October 2025, from 1 month to 30 years; the latest curve is highest and rises steadily from the short end out to 20 years, while the year-ago curve slopes down inside two years"/><figcaption>Treasury yield curve: month end (October 30, the last trading day of October), three months earlier, one year earlier.</figcaption></figure>

| Tenor | 2026-10-30 | 2026-07-31 | 2025-10-31 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1M | 4.06% | 3.78% | 4.06% |
| 3M | 4.16% | 3.83% | 3.89% |
| 6M | 4.25% | 3.98% | 3.79% |
| 1Y | 4.36% | 4.08% | 3.70% |
| 2Y | 4.56% | 4.28% | 3.60% |
| 5Y | 4.71% | 4.45% | 3.71% |
| 7Y | 4.80% | 4.59% | 3.89% |
| 10Y | 4.94% | 4.75% | 4.11% |
| 20Y | 5.34% | 5.28% | 4.65% |
| 30Y | 5.31% | 5.27% | 4.67% |
| **2s10s spread** | **0.38** | **0.47** | **0.51** |
| **3m10y spread** | **0.78** | **0.92** | **0.22** |

(Spreads in percentage points.)

**A year ago and today, the 1-month yield is the same 4.06%. Same starting point, opposite slope.** A year ago, yields fell all the way from 1 month to 2 years: 4.06%, 3.89%, 3.79%, 3.70%, 3.60%. The market was saying short rates would be lower in the future than today. That was a curve waiting for cuts. Today the same stretch rises all the way: 4.06%, 4.16%, 4.25%, 4.36%, 4.56%. Now the market is saying short rates will be higher than today. This is a curve waiting for hikes. The front of the curve isn't a thermometer. It's the route map the market has drawn for the central bank, and over one year that map was flipped over.

**The 2-year moved the most over the year**, up 0.96 points, more than the 10-year's 0.83. That's why 2s10s narrowed from 0.51 to 0.38, while the 3-month rose only 0.27 and 3m10y actually widened from 0.22 to 0.78. One spread narrowing while the other widens isn't a contradiction: the very shortest end is pinned by today's policy rate, and the most sensitive part is the "what happens over the next year or two" stretch.

**The last three months were a bear flattener**: the short end (2-year +0.28) rose faster than the long end (10-year +0.19, 30-year +0.04). That fits section 1. Energy pushes headline inflation up, and the market revises the near-term rate path first while its long-run view barely moves. One more detail: the 20-year (5.34%) sits slightly above the 30-year (5.31%), a small hump at the far end. That's true at the two most recent dates; a year ago the 30-year was slightly above the 20-year.

## 3. Spot bitcoin ETFs: two months of inflows, a small bite given back

<figure class="bls-diagram"><img src="/datapack/2026-10/etf.svg" alt="Bar chart of monthly net flows into spot bitcoin ETFs, November 2025 to October 2026: steady outflows early in the year, the largest outflow in June 2026, inflows in August and September, a small outflow in October"/><figcaption>US spot bitcoin ETF monthly net flows, $ billions.</figcaption></figure>

| Month | Net flow ($M) |
|---|---|
| 2025-11 | -3,467 |
| 2025-12 | -1,092 |
| 2026-01 | -1,605 |
| 2026-02 | -207 |
| 2026-03 | +1,316 |
| 2026-04 | +2,022 |
| 2026-05 | -2,406 |
| 2026-06 | -4,510 |
| 2026-07 | +173 |
| 2026-08 | +3,539 |
| 2026-09 | +2,648 |
| 2026-10 | -516 |
| **Last 12 months** | **-4,105** |
| **Farside full-table total (since launch)** | **+57,048** |

Seven of the last twelve months were net outflows and five were net inflows, for a combined outflow of about $4.1 billion. October's -$516 million is small, but it interrupted roughly $6.2 billion of inflows across August and September.

Reading this table means separating two things. ETF flows aren't "confidence." They're changes in who holds the asset. In an inflow month, new money is taking shares off the market. In an outflow month, existing holders are turning shares back into cash. June's -$4.5 billion was the biggest handover of the past year. July came in near zero, and inflows resumed in August and September: after the outflow came a pause before any rebound. October's small outflow is the first test. If November is negative too, the August and September inflows look more like a refill than the start of a new wave. Cumulative net inflows since launch are still about $57 billion. That base is thick, and a year's $4.1 billion of net outflows has only shaved the surface.

## 4. Hyperscaler capex: $200 billion in one quarter

<figure class="bls-diagram"><img src="/datapack/2026-10/capex.svg" alt="Stacked bar chart of quarterly capex at Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, Q4 2024 to Q3 2026, with the total rising from about $72 billion to about $202 billion"/><figcaption>Quarterly capex at Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta (cash flow statement basis, $ billions).</figcaption></figure>

| Company | 2026Q2 | 2026Q3 | YoY (vs 2025Q3) | QoQ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | 35.80 | 39.37 | +103% | +10% |
| Alphabet | 44.92 | 51.36 | +114% | +14% |
| Amazon | 54.21 | 77.26 | +120% | +43% |
| Meta | 30.12 | 33.94 | +80% | +13% |
| **Four-company total** | **165.05** | **201.93** | **+108%** | **+22%** |

($ billions. All four have reported Q3 2026, so the total is a complete quarter. Microsoft's fiscal year doesn't match the calendar; its quarter ending September 30 is placed in 2026Q3.)

The four spent a combined $201.9 billion in Q3, against $97.3 billion in the same quarter a year earlier: more than double. Amazon grew fastest, jumping from $54.2 billion to $77.3 billion in one quarter, more than 40% quarter on quarter. On its own it accounts for about $23 billion of the quarter's $36.9 billion increase in the total.

What matters most in this table isn't the level but the direction. Across eight quarters, the total was roughly flat only once, in Q1 2025 ($72.3 billion → $71.9 billion). Every other quarter it went up, and in each of the last two quarters it added more than $35 billion. Capex is a race no one dares to stop first. If you pause, your rivals won't; if your rivals don't pause, your pause becomes falling behind. It's a self-reinforcing loop, where today's spending supplies the reason for tomorrow's. Over the same year, the curve in section 2 shifted up by close to a point across the middle, which raises the return hurdle all this spending has to clear. The dearer money gets, the less cheap the race becomes. But nothing in the data so far shows any of the four slowing down because of it.

## Three things to watch next month

1. **October core CPI month on month**: all four core components sped up in September. If they speed up again in October, the second-round effect goes from "showing its face" to "established."
2. **Whether the 2-year or the 10-year runs faster**: if 2s10s keeps narrowing, the market is still adding near-term hikes; if it starts to widen, the pressure is moving out to the long end.
3. **Whether ETFs post a second straight month of outflows in November**: that decides whether August and September were a new allocation or a refill.

## Sources and definitions

- **Inflation**: BLS CPI-U, downloaded via FRED. Year-on-year rates use the non-seasonally-adjusted series; month-on-month rates use the seasonally adjusted series (consistent with how BLS reports them). Latest reading is September 2026.
- **Treasury curve**: US Treasury daily yield curve, downloaded via FRED (last available trading day for each tenor); month end is 2026-10-30. Spreads are 10-year minus 2-year and 10-year minus 3-month.
- **Spot bitcoin ETFs**: Farside Investors daily net flows for US spot bitcoin ETFs ($ millions), summed by month by our script; the cumulative figure is the sum of Farside's full table.
- **Hyperscaler capex**: SEC EDGAR XBRL (the "purchases of property and equipment" line in each company's 10-Q/10-K cash flow statement), with single quarters derived by subtracting year-to-date figures; quarters are mapped to calendar quarters by fiscal quarter end date. Note: this excludes principal payments on finance leases, so it runs slightly below each company's own "capex" figure (for example, Meta's 2026Q3 is $33.9 billion on this basis versus $34.9 billion on the company's basis including finance leases). Amazon uses the "purchases of productive assets" line.
- Year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter changes, totals and spread changes are calculated directly from the data above.
