Wednesday, November 11, US Eastern. Nvidia closed up nearly 3% during the regular session, a breather after days of headlines about a board investigation into its CEO. Less than an hour after the bell, the reports landed one after another: the board was preparing for the possibility that Jensen Huang would leave. After hours, the day's gain vanished and then some. Measured from October 27, the most watched company in the world has lost a fifth of its value in a little over two weeks.

And yet the dip-buying chorus only got louder. The line everyone reaches for: **Nvidia's forward P/E is now the lowest in more than a decade, far cheaper than the S&P 500 as a whole — chips don't stop selling because the CEO changes, so this is a margin of safety.** The product roadmap is mapped out years ahead. The orders are there. The profits are there. It sounds like a textbook sentiment overshoot. The only question is: **what, exactly, is sitting in the denominator of that temptingly low P/E?**

Here is the conclusion up front; every section below explains it: **a low multiple is not a discounted price. It is a discounted future.**

## 1. The denominator is a forecast

[Back in July](/en/posts/2026-07-09-cheap-is-not-margin/) the argument was that cheap tells you whether something is expensive, never whether it will rise. This piece takes one step further. The question here is not whether cheap can be trusted, but how the "cheap" got calculated in the first place.

A P/E ratio is price divided by earnings. Both sides sound like facts. Only the numerator is.

The price is today's trade, printed and settled. But the denominator of a *forward* P/E is the next twelve months of earnings — **a number that hasn't happened yet, a number an analyst forecast on your behalf.** It is not profit written in a ledger. It is profit written in an assumption.

Check it against Nvidia's own numbers. For the quarter that ended in late July, it earned $2.22 per share on an adjusted basis. **Assume it simply stands still and earns that much every quarter from here:** a year is four of those, and Wednesday's close of $173.73 is roughly twenty times that year of earnings. That multiple is my own arithmetic under a no-growth assumption, not anyone's published figure. And twenty times is nowhere near the cheapest in a decade.

So where does "the cheapest in a decade" come from? **From growth.** The low multiple everyone is quoting already has a big slab of future growth loaded into its denominator: for the quarter that just ended and will be reported next Tuesday, the company itself guided to $108 billion in revenue, more than a tenth above the quarter before, and the analyst models keep stacking growth on top of that. In other words, **this "cheap" is borrowed from next year's growth.**

That explains how the cheapness is calculated. It doesn't explain the obvious objection: if the products and the orders are all still there, how can a change of CEO touch next year's earnings?

## 2. A forecast is not a point. It's a fan

Because a forecast is never a point. It is a fan.

Picture next year's earnings as a folding fan. The handle is held today; the ribs spread out into the future. The middle rib is the consensus estimate in the analysts' models. The ribs on either side are everything that could happen if things go well or badly. **When the fan is folded tight, the middle rib is credible. When the fan is spread wide, the middle rib, however handsome, is just one possibility among many.**

Key-person risk doesn't move the middle rib. It changes the angle of the fan. This quarter's profit won't change; the chips still ship, and next Tuesday's report will very likely still look good. But a company's earnings several years out don't grow automatically out of a roadmap. They are strung together by a chain of decisions: whether to pull the next product generation in by another year, where scarce capacity goes, how the next multiyear deal with a giant customer gets negotiated, which ecosystem bet to make. **The tempo of those decisions is itself an asset, and it isn't on the balance sheet.**

So when a board starts talking about succession, the middle rib doesn't budge, but the fan snaps open. **The P/E fell not because the price got marked down, but because the "E" did.** The numerator is falling while the denominator hasn't been updated yet — part of the low multiple is nothing more than the lag between two numbers that refresh at different speeds.

That shows why the fan opens. It leaves out the part that matters most: once it opens, does it keep opening on its own?

## 3. In a rally the fan folds itself; in a selloff it opens itself

It does. For a company like Nvidia, the share price is one of the ribs of the fan.

**When the stock rises:** ① the equity in employees' hands keeps getting more valuable, so the best engineers have no reason to leave; ② the company can use ever-pricier stock to invest in customers and bind the ecosystem, locking in future orders early; ③ the tighter those orders are locked, the more confidently analysts raise their forecasts, the fan folds tighter, and the stock climbs again. **Growth makes the price credible, and the price makes the growth credible.** Left foot stepping on right, the fan folds tighter and tighter.

**When the stock falls:** ①′ employees' equity shrinks, and competitors' recruiting calls start getting answered; ②′ using stock to bind the ecosystem gets more expensive, and customers start to hesitate before putting multiyear bets on a company that may be changing captains; ③′ analysts start haircutting the out-years in their models, the fan opens wider, and the stock gets pushed lower. **At that point, the decline is manufacturing the reasons for the next decline.**

This is why "cheap" is a margin of safety on the way up and can be the entrance to a trap on the way down. **The same P/E can have growth standing behind a folded fan, or a question standing behind an open one.** Fundamentally, the multiple is an output. What sets it is how sure the market is about the growth — this is reflexivity, not arithmetic.

## 4. What to watch, and when this framework breaks

So instead of arguing over whether the multiple is the lowest in fifteen years or twenty, watch the fan itself. The earnings report next Tuesday, November 17, is the first chance to fold it back: the question is not whether this quarter beats — that is close to a given — but what the guidance for the next quarter says, and whether management can say one clear sentence about succession. **Earnings set the numerator. Certainty sets the fan.**

The framework has limits. If the board's investigation wraps up quickly with a clean, final answer either way, and the product cadence proves it really doesn't hinge on one person — the roadmap genuinely is laid out years ahead — the fan could fold back faster than it opened, and today's low multiple will look like a gift in hindsight. **The framework explains why "cheap" can't stand on its own. It doesn't predict when the fan closes.**

## 5. It's the future that got marked down

In the end, a multiple is a ratio of two numbers, and only one of them belongs to today. A stock that never grows can be expensive at ten times earnings; a stock that keeps compounding can be cheap at thirty. A low price only tells you that you're paying less. A credible future is what tells you that you'll get paid.

A company is a complex system, and its profit is the output of distributed computation — thousands of engineers, suppliers and customers moving to the same beat. What the market pays for was never this quarter's profit. It's the confidence that the system will keep computing. **When the center of that system wobbles, none of the compute disappears. The confidence does.**

**A low multiple is not a discounted price. It is a discounted future.** Once that clicks, it's clear how the same night and the same unchanged earnings forecast can map to two prices a long way apart: the numerator held still, and the fan opened.

<p class="kicker">The price belongs to today; the earnings belong to tomorrow. When the multiple gets cheap, it's tomorrow that took the markdown.</p>
