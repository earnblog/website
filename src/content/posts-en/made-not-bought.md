GDP doesn't measure how much you bought. It measures where it was made.

On Thursday (October 29, Eastern), the advance estimate of U.S. third-quarter GDP came out: 3.4% growth at an annual rate. The line that jumped off the table was imports. That item alone subtracted two and a half percentage points from growth. A tidy conclusion spread fast: imports held the economy back, and if America hadn't bought so much foreign stuff, it would have grown faster. But the same report carried another number. Look only at what American households and businesses spent themselves, consumption plus business and residential fixed investment, and growth was 4.9%. **The economy was buying things a lot faster than it was making them.** So who actually "dragged away" those two and a half points?

"Imports drag on growth" is an easy thing to say, and a textbook case of linear thinking that confuses bookkeeping with cause and effect. It assumes a minus sign is a loss: the more you import, the smaller the economy, so buy less from abroad and GDP gets bigger. It sounds self-evident. In fact it rests on a misreading of a table.

Start with how that table keeps its books. GDP is trying to answer one question: how much was produced inside the United States this quarter. But the statisticians can't tell where each purchase or each machine was made at the moment it's recorded, so they work in two steps. First they record everything that was spent, consumption, investment, inventories, wherever it came from. Then they subtract the part bought from abroad as "imports." **The minus sign on imports isn't a penalty. It's a return: it sends back whatever got recorded but wasn't made in America.** A server shipped in from overseas is a plus in equipment investment and a minus in imports. Plus one, minus one, net effect on GDP: zero. It made the U.S. economy neither bigger nor smaller.

That explains what the minus is. It doesn't yet explain where this quarter's pluses landed.

The report gives the clue itself. Equipment investment this quarter was "led by information processing equipment (mainly computers and peripheral equipment)," and that item alone contributed 0.86 percentage points to growth. In other words, buying computers and servers held up about a quarter of the headline. Inventories added another 1.6 points; the warehouses were filling. Much of this is likely tied to the AI data centers being built out. The report doesn't say so; that's my inference. Put the three lines together and the picture is clear: **machines and goods poured in from abroad, some into server halls and some into warehouses, and what was sent back in the imports column is exactly what had been recorded in the investment and inventory columns.**

If plus one and minus one make zero, why care? Because 3.4% and 4.9% answer two different questions, and markets often use the wrong one. 3.4% answers "how much was made in America." 4.9% answers "how much Americans spent." The first is the supply side's report card; the second is demand's thermometer. For corporate orders, for hiring and for inflation pressure, the second is usually the more direct rope. **Reading hot demand as weak growth means mistaking a return slip for a loss statement.**

There's a mirror here. When imports surge: ① demand is strong and lots gets shipped in; ② the imports column subtracts a lot; ③ GDP looks weak, when in fact demand is running a fever. Flip it, and when imports collapse: ①′ demand may have cooled, or last quarter's rush to import may simply be unwinding; ②′ the imports column subtracts little; ③′ GDP looks strong, when demand may actually be cooling. The first half of 2025 ran exactly this play. Around the arrival of tariffs, first came a stampede to import, then a sudden drop-off. First-quarter GDP barely moved, then the second quarter snapped back hard. Most of that whiplash was this one minus sign swinging back and forth. **The minus sign swung; demand didn't necessarily swing with it.**

At bottom, GDP is a certificate of origin, not a shopping receipt. Confuse the two and you arrive at a strange conclusion: to make the economy stronger, just buy less from abroad. But demand is a distributed network. A company building a data center buys the best machine it can get delivered fastest, wherever it was made. It doesn't stop because a statistical table grew a minus sign. Try to steer that network with an accounting convention and what you get is a harder-to-read number, not a stronger economy.

Here's how any reader can approach the next GDP report: line up three numbers side by side, headline growth, real final sales to private domestic purchasers, and the contribution from imports. The further apart the first two are, the more the quarter's story is written in the minus sign rather than in the economy.

This framework has its limits. Plus one, minus one only nets to zero within the same quarter. If imported goods don't sell that quarter, they sit in inventory and come out the next, so two quarters' figures pull against each other. More importantly, an accounting identity says nothing about causation. If foreign goods really did displace things that would otherwise have been made in America, that loss is real. It just shows up as domestic production that never grew, not in the minus sign. And this is only the advance estimate; it gets revised again at the end of November.

In the end, this is a small lesson in humility about measurement. Every statistic is a cross-section that people slice out of an enormous network so they can make sense of it, and where you make the cut decides what the answer looks like. Reading an accounting minus sign as an economic loss is like mistaking the marks on a ruler for the thing being measured. The ruler isn't wrong. The person holding it forgot which dimension it measures.

<p class="kicker">The minus sign on imports is not a drag but a return; GDP never measured what you bought, only where it was made.</p>
