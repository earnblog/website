On Thursday, November 5 (US Eastern), the PHLX Semiconductor Index lost about 12% in a single session, AMD and Micron each fell roughly 17%, and the whole Nasdaq slid into correction territory along with them. The trigger, on its face, was light: after the election results, the market bet that new data center projects would face more permitting and more resistance, and some analysts estimated that a meaningful slice of the computing capacity planned for 2028 could be pushed back.

So one line quickly took over every trading chat: **not a single restriction has been enacted, not a single order has been cancelled, and the chipmakers just delivered pristine quarterly results. The fundamentals haven't changed a word. This is a sentiment selloff, and it will bounce back.** It sounds calm and reasonable. But it quietly assumes one thing: as long as nothing is lost, only delayed, the price should not move much. That is exactly where the problem lies. For a growth stock, how different, in price, is "arriving late" from "partly lost"?

## 1. Nothing changed, so why a 12% drop?

Start by taking the word "fundamentals" apart. Those who say the fundamentals haven't changed are holding today's report card: revenue, profit, backlog. But a growth stock has never been priced off today's report card. It buys a **schedule**: how much ships in which year, how much cash arrives in which year, how big the business gets by when. Today's profit fills only one small cell of that schedule. The bulk sits further right, two, three, five years out.

So the news that data centers may be slowed does not need to cancel a single order to change the schedule. It does not cross out a cell; it shifts the whole row one slot to the right. The report card did not change. The schedule did. **A valuation is not a number, it is a schedule.** Change the schedule and you have changed the price.

That explains how the stock can fall when nothing has happened. It does not yet explain why a one-slot shift should cost anything. The money is not smaller, it just arrives later.

## 2. Why one year late is not "a bit late"

Because time is not free. A dollar today and a dollar next year are not the same dollar: between them sits a year of interest, a year of opportunity, a year of things that can go wrong. Bringing future money back to today means applying a discount, and the size of that discount is the price of time.

Take the crudest example, with a number I picked purely for illustration: if the market demands a 10% annual return from this kind of company, then a stream of cash that arrives one year late is worth only a little over 90% of what it was today. **Nothing is missing, it is simply a year late, and nearly a tenth of the value is already gone.** That is not sentiment. That is arithmetic.

And right now time is expensive. The upper bound of the Fed's policy rate is still 4%, and the market is still debating whether the next move is a hike. When risk-free money earns that much in a year, the cost of waiting rises with the tide. The pricier time is, the deeper a delay cuts. In that sense, **a delay is not just later, it is a markdown.**

But if it were only arithmetic, Nvidia's 9% drop looks roughly like "one year late." AMD and Micron fell 17%, close to double. What is the extra slice?

## 3. Chips are not gold; they expire

The extra slice exists because, for the people selling chips, a delay is often more than receiving the same money a year later.

Gold sold a year late is still the same gold. A chip is not. Semiconductors move in generations, and each generation has its window. A chip meant for the 2028 wave of server halls may, if those halls open a year late, find the customer already weighing the next generation. The late order may no longer be the same order: a different part, a different price, maybe a different supplier. Memory, a business that earns its money from tight supply, is even more exposed. Capacity built for a shortage that runs into a delay may come online just as the shortage ends.

So for the sellers of compute, a delay stacks two discounts: one for time and one for **expiry**. The first is arithmetic; the second is perishable goods. One cut lands on the timing, another on the price.

So will the revised schedule simply stay where it is?

## 4. The price rewrites the schedule

No. The schedule is not written in advance and left for the market to read; the market's reading feeds back into it.

On the way up: ① chip and compute stocks rise; ② capital markets fund data center projects more cheaply, with easy bond deals and flattering valuations; ③ projects break ground on time or early; ④ orders are delivered, proving the price in ① was right. **The price finances the schedule, and the schedule testifies for the price.**

In reverse, on the way down: ①′ stocks fall and risk appetite shrinks; ②′ financing for data center projects gets dearer and harder, and those already borrowing to build think twice; ③′ some projects really do slip; ④′ orders really do arrive late, proving the fall in ①′ was "right." **What starts as a bet on a delay makes the delay more real.** That is reflexivity: the market does not just price the schedule, it is one of the schedule's financiers.

The flaw in "the fundamentals haven't changed, sentiment will pass" is not that it misreads today. It is that it assumes the fundamentals will stand still after today, waiting for the price to come back.

## 5. What to watch, and where this view breaks

Down at the level of things you can observe, stop staring at daily moves and watch whether the schedule itself is being rewritten. First, the cloud giants' capex guidance next quarter: maintained, raised, or "shifted right." Second, financing terms for data center projects: are credit spreads on that debt tightening or widening? Third, the language on delivery timing in chipmakers' results. A "deferral" and a "cancellation" are different things; the first applies one discount, the second applies two.

This view has limits. First, if demand far exceeds supply, a delay just lengthens the queue. Customers would rather wait than switch, the late order is the same order, the expiry discount does not apply, and prices recover quickly. Second, the time discount is very sensitive to rates; if rates fall meaningfully from here, the same delay cuts shallower. Third, when the market is trading panic rather than cash flows, short-term stampedes magnify any discounting arithmetic, and short-term bounces erase it.

## 6. Time is the one counterparty that does not haggle

Ultimately, people who say "the fundamentals haven't changed" make a very human mistake: they assume that as long as the thing is still there, the value is still there. In a complex system, value has never been only *what* you have; it is also *when* you have it. The same order arriving today, next year, or the year after is three different prices, and the gap between them is set by no single person but by the collective bid of all capital for time.

You can negotiate with a counterparty, maneuver around a regulator, take a punt against market mood. You cannot haggle with time. Once the schedule shifts one slot to the right, the system settles the account immediately: not when the order is actually lost, but the moment the delay becomes visible. Humility before the market means admitting this: **every "it will pay off eventually" in your hands has already been marked down once in the price.**

<p class="kicker">A delay is not just later, it is a markdown; a valuation is not a number, it is a schedule.</p>
