A rate hike is not a decision. It's a curve.

The Fed announces its October decision on Wednesday afternoon, Eastern time, and nearly everyone is betting it stands pat this time and saves the next hike for December. So a comfortable line is making the rounds: no hike this time, markets can finally breathe. But at Tuesday's close (October 27), lending money to the U.S. government for two years paid 4.70%, while the rate banks actually charge each other overnight, the number the Fed really targets, sat at 3.88%. **Two-year money is costing more than 80 basis points more than overnight money.** Nobody has held a meeting in weeks. So who added those 80-odd basis points?

Start with what's wrong with the popular line. It assumes that rates are set in a meeting room, and that if nobody meets, nothing moves. This is classic button thinking: the belief that behind the whole web of interest rates sits a single switch, and if the switch isn't pressed, the water doesn't flow. In reality, the only point the Fed presses directly is the overnight rate. Companies borrowing, families buying homes and the Treasury issuing debt all use an entire curve, from one month to thirty years. **The number announced in the meeting room ratifies the curve; it is not the curve's source.**

So who sets the curve? The 2-year yield is the price you demand today for lending your money for two years. Whoever quotes it doesn't just look at tonight; they walk through every meeting over the next two years in their head, how many hikes, when, where it stops, then flatten that whole path into one number. So 4.70% is nobody's quote. It's a number computed jointly, on the same network, by countless bond traders, pension funds and bank treasurers. Eighty-odd basis points is roughly the length of three standard hikes. That's my own subtraction of one number from the other, and it also contains the extra compensation lenders demand for tying money up longer, so it isn't all hike expectations. But the direction is unmistakable: **the market has already walked most of the next few steps for the Fed.**

That explains why the curve sits above the policy rate. It doesn't yet explain the more important question: does the curve only ever follow the Fed?

It doesn't. Go back to September. At the start of the month, the 2-year was at 4.39%; by the day before the hike, it was already 4.67%. **The Fed hadn't moved, and the curve had already risen nearly 30 basis points, more than the hike the Fed then delivered.** By hike day, the real tightening was done; the meeting room just stamped a seal on something that had already happened.

Then look forward. After the hike, the 2-year briefly spiked to 4.92%. Over the following month, the Fed didn't meet once and the policy rate didn't budge, yet the 2-year slid back to 4.70% on its own. Nobody announced any easing. The market loosened a notch all by itself.

Put those two stretches side by side and you get a mirror. When the curve rises: ① traders expect hikes and the 2-year climbs; ② mortgages, corporate bonds and auto loans reprice and borrowing gets dearer; ③ dearer funding weighs on investment and spending. The tightening happens before the meeting. Flip it, and when the curve falls: ①′ traders sense the tightest point is passing and the 2-year slides; ②′ borrowing costs ease and valuations lift; ③′ looser financial conditions prop up demand and asset prices. The easing, too, happens before the meeting. **In both directions, the meeting room is one step late.**

And this isn't a one-way street. It's a loop. The Fed's words move the curve, the curve changes financial conditions, financial conditions change the economy, and the economic data lands back on the Fed's desk to decide what it says next. ① drives ②, and ② rewrites ①. In that sense the Fed is not the person turning the tap. It's more like the biggest rock in the tide: it really can redirect the flow, but the tide itself rises and falls on the combined expectations of millions. That's why "no hike this time" carries almost no information. It's already in the price. The real information is which way the statement's wording nudges the curve tomorrow.

There's a simple thing any reader can watch: the gap between the 2-year yield and the effective fed funds rate. When the gap widens, the market is tightening on the Fed's behalf. When it narrows, the market is easing on its behalf. When the decision comes out, don't rush to ask "did they hike?" Look at whether that gap is wider or narrower the next day.

This framework has its limits. The 2-year isn't pure hike expectations. Inflation compensation, Treasury supply and sudden safe-haven buying all push it around. In a panic the 2-year can plunge and look like "the market cutting for the Fed," when really it's just people looking for shelter from the rain. And the curve is an expectation, not a promise. It can be wrong, and it is often wrong in perfect unison. What I can be sure of is who moves first, not who turns out right in the end.

In the end, this is a small lesson in humility about who sets the price. We like to picture interest rates as one person's decision at one table, because a decision is easy to understand, easy to report and easy to bet on. But the real rate is computed every day by a network across countless trades. The Fed is the heaviest node in that network, but it is still a node. Everyone waiting to "breathe" this time already got their relief over the past month. Nobody held a meeting to announce it.

<p class="kicker">Rates don't live in the meeting room; they live in the curve, and the meeting room only announces a step the curve priced long ago.</p>
