**Negative cash flow isn't burning money. It's a change of payer.**

After the close on Wednesday, October 21 (Eastern), Meta reported its third quarter: revenue growth still strong, the ad business showing no cracks. But on another statement, the cash it brought in from operations this quarter was $32.79 billion, while what it spent on data centers, chips and servers was $34.91 billion — **it earned less cash than it spent.** Free cash flow — the cash a business generates minus what it pours into capital spending — was comfortably positive in the same quarter a year ago; this quarter it flipped negative. On Thursday the stock closed down almost 7%, and the verdict was instant: the market is punishing AI cash burn.

"Cash burn" points the question in the wrong direction. **It assumes the money is leaving the company's own pocket.** But when a company doesn't earn enough cash to pay its bills, the bill doesn't disappear. Someone else simply pays it. This piece answers one question: when Meta's cash flow turned negative, what actually changed? Not "it's spending more," but "who is paying."

Start with two bills. In the first nine months of 2025, Meta spent $26.2 billion buying back its own stock — money flowing from the company back to shareholders. In the same nine months of 2026, buybacks were zero; not a single share. Over the same stretch it issued about $24.9 billion of long-term debt. **The flow of money reversed completely: last year the company handed its surplus back to shareholders; this year it is borrowing from the bond market.** And the foot is still on the gas: backing out from the low end of its full-year capex range, the fourth quarter alone implies close to $50 billion of spending — my own subtraction from company figures, not a quarterly guide Meta issued.

That explains what changed. It doesn't yet explain why it matters. It matters because when the payer changes, the direction of the whole system's feedback changes with it.

When a company invests out of its own cash flow: ① strong ad revenue means more cash and more data centers; ② when ad revenue weakens, cash shrinks and spending contracts on its own; ③ how much it spends is capped by how much it earns. **That is negative feedback with a built-in brake.** However wild the vision, it can't break through the bottom of its own wallet.

When it invests with someone else's money: ①′ a high stock price and a compelling AI story make the bond market eager to lend at tolerable rates; ②′ borrowed money becomes orders flowing to chipmakers, power suppliers and builders, lifting their revenue and their stock prices; ③′ those suppliers' glowing earnings in turn "prove" that AI demand is booming, the story gets better and borrowing gets easier; ④′ so spending climbs again. **The brake has been removed and replaced with a lever that pumps itself.** Spending is no longer capped by what the company earns, but by what others are willing to lend.

In that sense, the sign on free cash flow isn't a report card; it's a switch. When it's positive, AI spending is a company's internal affair. When it's negative, it becomes the whole credit market's affair. A little over two weeks ago I wrote that [AI investment isn't growth immune to interest rates — it's a bidder for them](/en/posts/capex-is-a-bidder). That was about the demand side. Now we've reached the funding side: with the 10-year Treasury yield closing near 5% on Friday, AI spending has to take its own rate of return and stand in the same line at the same counter as everyone else who borrows.

So what, at bottom, was Thursday's drop? Not the market suddenly discovering that "AI doesn't make money" — the revenue growth is right there. It was price recalculating one thing: the gas pedal on this machine has moved from the company's own foot to the bond market's. **When the payer changes, the power to set the price changes with it.** An investment plan that answers only to its own cash flow carries one risk: low returns. An investment plan that lives on borrowing carries one more: not being able to borrow.

This framework has limits. Meta still holds more than $80 billion in cash and marketable securities; one negative quarter is a long way from "can't borrow," and its revenue growth suggests the money is, for now, buying real business. Negative free cash flow has appeared in the history of many companies, often as the starting point of a long-term winner rather than the end. All I can be sure of is that the direction of feedback has changed; I can't say on what day this positive loop will stop. What's worth watching isn't the sign in any single quarter, but two things: whether its debt gets more expensive or cheaper as it borrows more, and whether its suppliers' orders are accelerating or slowing.

In the end, this is a small lesson in humility about who keeps the books. However strong a company is, on its own it can only marshal its own cash flow. The moment it reaches out to the market to borrow, it plugs itself into a much larger network — countless lenders, countless price-setters, all computing its future at once. That network can lift it higher than it could go alone. And one day it can hit the brake before the company itself would.

<p class="kicker">Spend your own money and the brake is under your own foot; spend someone else's and it's under theirs — when cash flow turns negative, what changes was never the money, but whose foot is on the brake.</p>
