**An earnings report isn't news. It's a receipt.**

On Thursday, October 15 (Eastern), TSMC turned in a third-quarter report card with almost nothing to fault: revenue of $46.72 billion, above the top of the range the company itself had guided to; net income up 68.7% year over year — in other words, it earned close to seventy percent more than in the same quarter last year. Yet its U.S.-listed ADR closed down 1.8% that day and fell another 2.3% on Friday. From its October 5 closing high of $485.80 to Friday's close of $412.61, it has given up roughly 15%.

There are two popular readings. One says the market has lost its mind — it won't even reward a report card this good. The other says a drop like this must mean something is buried in the numbers. **Both make the same mistake: they treat the earnings report as news.** News is something you didn't already know. This quarter's growth was something price already knew — and had already paid for. This piece answers a single question: why does price move down at the very moment good news lands?

Start with what "already paid for" means. A stock price isn't a weighing of today's profits; it's a prepayment on future ones. In the first trading days of October, TSMC's ADR climbed to its highest close of the month. The third quarter hadn't been booked yet, but buyers were already staking real money on it: this quarter will be great, and so will the next. **Price pays first; the earnings report delivers later.** When the report landed on October 15, the goods arrived as ordered — even with a little extra. But the money had been paid ten days earlier. Nobody pays twice for the same shipment on delivery day.

That explains why good news doesn't lift the price. It doesn't explain why the price falls. The answer lives one layer down: **price doesn't buy growth — it buys the acceleration of growth.** Revenue this quarter rose 16.2% over the previous quarter. The company guided fourth-quarter revenue to $52.3–54.3 billion; at the midpoint, that's about 14% above the third quarter — my own arithmetic from the guidance midpoint, not a company figure. Growth is still fast, startlingly fast. But it has shifted from "each quarter faster than the last" to "each quarter about as fast as the last." Think of driving: the car is still doing 75, but the foot is no longer pressing harder on the gas. Passengers feel no slowdown. Anyone watching the dashboard sees the needle stop climbing.

Which leads to the crucial layer: why is price so sensitive to acceleration? Because price and expectations are locked in a bootstrap loop. ① Growth accelerates and the stock rises. ② The rising stock pushes everyone to raise next quarter's expectations. ③ Higher expectations mean the next report has to be even better just to justify today's price. ④ So a next quarter that is merely "as good as this one" reads, through price's eyes, as "not good enough." **The growth required just to keep the stock from falling is itself growing faster than exponentially.** That isn't a market malfunction. It's the arithmetic of prepayment: the more you prepay, the higher the bar on delivery day.

Put the loop at both ends of a trend and it becomes plain. Early in a trend: ①′ nobody dares to prepay, so the price is cheap; ②′ the report blows past everyone's imagination; ③′ price gets pushed by the report, and every good quarter is a repricing — **at this stage, earnings are news.** Late in a trend: ① price has already prepaid several quarters ahead; ② the report merely checks whether the prepaid goods have arrived; ③ if the check passes, price doesn't move; if it reveals acceleration fading, price drifts lower — **at this stage, earnings are a receipt.** The same company, the same kind of great quarter, ignites the price early in a trend and late in a trend merely gets stamped "paid in full."

In that sense, the most important number on that October 15 report wasn't 68.7%. It was the quarterly growth rate easing from 16% to 14%. The first is the amount on the receipt. The second is the only real news about the future.

Of course, other forces were pulling this week too. The Nasdaq Composite fell about 4.5% from October 9 to October 16, including 2.1% on Friday alone, while oil spiked over the same stretch. How much of TSMC's slide was the receipt effect and how much was simply being dragged down with the broader market, the stock price alone can't tell you. That's this framework's weakness: it explains why good news stops lifting the price, but it can't cleanly split a decline into "expectations used up" and "outside shock." Only the next report can settle it — if acceleration picks back up next quarter and the price still won't rise, then what's pressing on it isn't the receipt. It's something else.

So what can an ordinary investor watch? Don't fixate on how high the year-over-year growth is — that's the amount on the receipt. Watch whether the quarterly growth rate is speeding up or slowing down. That's the only piece of information nobody has paid for yet.

Underneath all this is a very old kind of humility. We like to believe we "learn" something the moment an earnings report drops. But the market, a computing system made up of countless people, finished computing everything computable long before we read the numbers. What it leaves for latecomers is a receipt, and one question that hasn't been priced yet: next quarter, will the foot press any harder on the gas?

<p class="kicker">Price never pays twice for good news that already happened; what it pays for, always, is the bit of acceleration that hasn't happened yet.</p>
